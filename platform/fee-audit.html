Case Study 002 · Cost Modelling · Self-audit

The cost model was wrong
in form, not in value

Every strategy this platform has ever killed was killed on a cost bar. In September 2026 I found that the cost bar itself was miscomputed — not by a mistuned parameter, but by applying a rounding step in the wrong place. It made trading look more expensive than it is, by 1.14× at the money and up to 14× in the tails, and my own test suite was pinning the error in place.

01 Why the fee model is the most important line of code

On a prediction market the price is a probability, and before costs the expected change in price is zero. There is no drift to lend a strategy the benefit of the doubt, no buy-and-hold premium, no compounding to bail out a marginal edge. Every strategy is therefore evaluated as a single comparison: is the measured mispricing bigger than the round-trip cost?

Which means the cost function is not a detail at the edge of the model. It is one side of the only inequality that matters. Six strategy families in this project have been retired, and the majority of them were retired because a real, measurable effect was too small to clear the cost bar. If the bar is set too high, those verdicts are wrong in a direction that is invisible: nothing crashes, no test fails, and a research programme quietly concludes that a venue is more efficient than it is.

02 The published schedule, and what I had implemented

Kalshi's taker fee is quoted as

Exchange schedule

fee = roundup( M × 0.07 × C × P × (1 − P) )

with C the number of contracts, P the price in dollars, M a per-series multiplier, and the round-up to the next cent applied once, to the whole order.

What my pricer computed was a per-contract fee:

What was implemented

fee_per_contract = ceil( 0.07 × M × P × (1−P) × 100 ) / 100
total = C × fee_per_contract

The ceiling has moved inside the multiplication by C. Applied once to an order, the round-up costs at most one cent in total. Applied per contract and then multiplied, it costs up to a cent on every contract.

The two forms agree only when the per-contract fee happens to land exactly on a cent. Everywhere else the implemented version is strictly larger. The exchange's own worked example settles which is right: 100 contracts at $0.50 is documented as $1.75. The per-order form gives $1.75. The per-contract form gives $2.00.

03 What the corrected model says about the venue

Fixing the form does more than move a number — it exposes structure that the staircase was hiding. Divide the correct fee by the capital actually at risk:

Fee as a fraction of stake

fee / stake  =  0.07 · C · P(1−P)  /  (C · P)  =  0.07 · (1 − P)

The contract count cancels and the price mostly cancels. What is left is linear and decreasing: 6.65% of stake at p = 0.05, 3.5% at p = 0.50, and 0.35% at p = 0.95.

The ceiling adds a fixed per-order minimum on top of that rate, which is what makes small orders inefficient rather than merely small. At p = 0.95 a $1 order pays about 1.0% of stake, a $10 order 0.40%, and a $100 order 0.36%. For an account trading in single-dollar clips, order granularity is itself a cost line.

04 A second error, one layer down

Having written the corrected formula, I checked it against the exchange's worked example and it returned $1.76, not $1.75. The formula was right; the arithmetic was not. In binary floating point 0.07 × 100 × 0.5 × 0.5 evaluates to 1.7500000000000002, and a ceiling function does exactly what it is told with that.

Sweeping every order size from 1 to 200 contracts against every price from $0.01 to $0.99 — 19,800 cells — the float implementation disagrees with exact decimal arithmetic in 81 of them. Always by one cent, and always in the same direction: overstating. It is a small effect, and it is the same effect as the first one in miniature: a rounding step meeting a value that is a hair off a boundary. The published implementation uses decimal arithmetic.

Both errors point the same way

Neither mistake was random. A misplaced ceiling and a floating-point epsilon both round up, so both make trading look more expensive than it is. A cost model that errs high is the comfortable kind of wrong — it produces conservative verdicts, kills marginal strategies, and never announces itself. That is precisely why it survived for months in a codebase whose author was actively looking for errors.

05 Blast radius

An honest audit states what it invalidates. The corrected model changes conclusions in proportion to how close to the bar they were, and how far from the money they were priced.

Recomputing the MLB carry ledger under both models makes the size of the thing concrete on real fills: 260 contracts bought at an average of $0.84 owe $2.60 under the correct schedule and $3.24 under the implemented one — a 1.25× overstatement on an actual, settled order book, and 5% of that sleeve's entire gross P&L.

06 What the tests were doing

The detail I find most worth keeping is that eight test functions covered this function, carrying twenty-three assertions between them, and every one passed. They were not weak tests. They asserted the quadratic shape, the symmetry about the midpoint, clipping outside [0, 1], and that the vectorised path matched the scalar one — every property the implementation actually had.

One of them was named test_taker_fee_peaks_at_two_cents_atm, and it asserted fee(0.5) == 0.02. That is the bug, written down as a requirement and defended by a green check mark. The correct answer is 1.75¢.

The lesson

A test suite written from the implementation cannot falsify the implementation. Each of those tests encoded a property I believed the fee had, and every one of those beliefs came from the same reading of the schedule that produced the bug. The suite was internally consistent and externally wrong, and its green status was actively harmful: it made the function look like the settled part of the codebase.

What found the error was not a test. It was recomputing the exchange's own published worked example and getting a different number — a single external anchor, checked against the source rather than against my own understanding of the source. The corrected implementation is now pinned to that worked example as its first test case.

07 Remediation

  • Fee computed per order, with the ceiling applied once, in decimal arithmetic.
  • The exchange's published worked example is test case one; the float-vs-decimal sweep is test case two.
  • Every net-of-cost verdict tagged with the fee-model version it was computed under, so a future correction has a list of what to re-run rather than a search.
  • Wing-related conclusions marked void pending a re-run, rather than quietly inherited.
  • The maker-side fee is separately unresolved — the multiplier defaults to zero on the series in question, and the round-up granularity is decidable only from settled fills of my own.

The corrected implementation, with both tests, is in the companion repository. It is short enough to read in a minute and it is the single most load-bearing function in the whole platform.
