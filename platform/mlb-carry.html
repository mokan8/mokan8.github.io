A strategy that made money
and still had to be killed

MLB late-favourite carry ran live on Kalshi for fourteen days and finished up 2.4% on a $500 research bankroll. It was retired anyway — not because it lost, but because the backtest that justified it was conditioning on the outcome, and forty-five markets could never have told the difference.

01 The claim

Kalshi lists a market on every MLB game that pays $1 if a named team wins and $0 otherwise. Late in a game, a team that is comfortably ahead trades somewhere around 0.75–0.88. The hypothesis, written down before any money moved:

Pre-registered hypothesis

Late in-game MLB favourites priced 0.75–0.88 settle YES more often than the ask implies, so buying and holding to settlement is +EV net of the ask and Kalshi's quadratic taker fee.

EDGE SOURCE behavioural  ·  BAND mid 0.75–0.88  ·  EXIT hold to settlement

This is a deliberately humble claim. Event contracts resolve to 0 or 1 at a known time, so the price is a bounded martingale — before costs, E[Δprice] = 0 by no-arbitrage. Any claimed edge is therefore a strong claim: it has to beat a full round trip of frictions, and on Kalshi the taker fee is quadratic in price, peaking at p = 0.5. Buy-and-hold is the cheapest possible structure on this venue — it pays half a spread and one fee, with no exit, no queue, and no latency requirement. If an edge cannot survive there, it cannot survive anywhere on the venue.

The kill criterion was pre-registered too: all-in live net must clear zero with a bootstrap CI excluding it at a pre-registered sample size. It is worth noting up front that this is not the criterion that ended up killing the strategy. Something better did.

02 In-sample, robustness, and a sealed out-of-sample

The research followed the standard sequence. Entry candidates were defined on minute candles with a non-anticipative liquidity gate — spread at or below the cap, trailing five-minute volume above a floor, both computed only from information available at the candle's close. Costs were charged at the ask, plus the Kalshi taker fee. Outcomes were scored against settlement, never against the last candle close (a loser's final print averages 0.16, not 0 — markets stop trading before they resolve, and reading P&L off the last print quietly inflates every loss).

Trades within one game are one bet, so all intervals are event-level block bootstraps rather than per-trade t-statistics. The out-of-sample partition was sealed and run once.

At the time this read as two independent confirmations of the same effect across two different sports.

Worth holding onto

A tight IS→OOS agreement and a clean robustness sweep are the two things a research process is supposed to produce. Both were present here. Neither of them can see the defect that eventually killed this strategy, and Section 05 explains why that is structural rather than bad luck.

03 What actually shipped

The live scanner polls open MLB markets, filters to the validated band, checks the entry trigger, re-quotes immediately before acting, and sends an immediate-or-cancel limit order at the ask. One entry per game. Micro size, scaled off a $500 bankroll. A risk gate runs before every scan, and a single-writer fence prevents two scanner instances from double-entering the same event.

Two guards are worth calling out because they are the difference between live trading and a backtest replayed with real money. If the market leaves the band between listing and re-quote, the entry is skipped and logged as requote_out — an economically real miss, not a silent hole in the sample. If the live spread exceeds the backtest's liquidity cap, the entry is skipped as wide_spread: a wide live book is outside the validated sample, however tempting the mid looks.

The gap that mattered

The backtest entered on minutes_to_expiry ≤ 30. The live scanner entered on inning ≥ 8.

They were meant to be the same condition expressed in two data sources — the backtest had candles and a settlement timestamp, the live system had a play-by-play feed. They are not the same condition, and the difference is the whole case study. Minutes-to-expiry is not computable at decision time. A live scanner cannot know when a game will end, so it cannot trade the rule that was validated. That should have been the tell.

04 Fourteen days live

The strategy ran from 28 June to 11 July 2026. Every entry attempt was logged — fills and no-fills alike — because a fill log alone cannot measure the cost of the order structure.

Two thirds of the contracts were traded in the final week, at a hit rate of 83.3% against a breakeven of 84.4%. The sleeve was largest exactly when it was, for the first time, losing money per contract. That is not a coincidence and it is not bad luck — it is what position sizing driven by accumulating "confirmation" does when the confirmation is noise. It is the mechanism by which a strategy with no edge still manages to lose real money eventually.

Why the P&L could not settle anything

41 wins and 4 losses. For a strategy that wins ~90% of the time, essentially all the information about the edge lives in the losses — the wins are nearly guaranteed and carry almost no signal about whether the ask was too low. The effective sample size is 4, not 45.

This is also why a bootstrap CI has to be read carefully here: below a minimum loss count, resampling a nearly loss-free sample produces intervals that are provably overconfident, because many resamples contain no losses at all. The honest interval on the per-market mean is [−1.60¢, +14.27¢], which is 16 cents wide on a quantity whose entire plausible range is single digits.

05 The audit

On 11 July the trigger definitions went under audit. The question asked was not "does this backtest overfit?" but a different one: what does this entry condition condition on?

minutes_to_expiry is computed against the market's realised settlement time. So "entered within 30 minutes of expiry" is not a statement about game state — it is a statement that the game ended shortly after entry. For a team already leading, that is outcome information. Games that end quickly from that state are disproportionately games the favourite closed out. Comebacks and extra innings extend the clock, and are therefore silently excluded from the entry set. The trigger was quietly filtering out precisely the losses it was supposed to be measuring.

The test: re-run the strategy on a play-by-play join, using triggers that are computable at decision time. act_T is a cumulative active-minutes clock derived from the candles themselves; inning ≥ 8 is the trigger that actually shipped.

Late in-band MLB favourites are approximately fairly priced: hit rate 0.807 against an average ask of 0.827, which is within noise of the no-arbitrage prediction. After fees, the honest trigger is negative. The sealed +4.76¢ out-of-sample number was not optimistic — it was inadmissible.

The tennis sibling was worse, and instructive about why. A tennis match ends because someone won; baseball's fixed nine innings make "ends soon" less outcome-informative, which is why MLB's artifact was the smaller of the two. Substituting a non-anticipative activity clock that reaches the same median lateness as the biased trigger (31 minutes vs 26) turns +11.55¢ into −1.54¢. The entire measured edge was the conditioning.

Why every gate passed anyway

Robustness checks probe sensitivity to parameters. They cannot see a defect in a trigger's definition. All 27 perturbations were positive around a biased trigger; varying the threshold does not remove the conditioning, it just moves it.

A sealed out-of-sample split cannot detect it either. The bias lives in how the entry condition is defined, so both partitions inherit it equally and in the same direction. Out-of-sample discipline protects against overfitting to a particular sample; it offers no protection at all against a rule that is not computable at decision time. Only asking "what does this condition on?" finds this class of defect.

And the corollary that stung most: the MLB→tennis replication carried no information. It varied the sport but not the component that could be broken. A replication is evidence only if it varies the suspect component.

06 Retirement, and the trap underneath it

  • 2026-06-28
    First live fills. One contract per market, dry-run guard removed only after the order path was probed.
  • 2026-07-08 → 07-11
    Size ramps to 14 contracts. Hit rate slips below breakeven. Two scanner instances briefly duel — a forgotten laptop process against the deployed droplet — which is what motivated the single-writer fence.
  • 2026-07-11
    Lookahead audit. Live halted the same day. Tennis never deployed. MLB retired: the validating backtest is inadmissible, and the live ledger cannot distinguish −3¢ from +5¢ at this fill count.
  • 2026-07-30
    Docs reconciled. Verdicts written into the registry in full, including the numbers that made the case.

Retiring a strategy is not a note in a file — it is a state change in a running system, and this one had a sting in the tail. The platform runs a CUSUM decay detector on settled outcomes: a one-sided cumulative sum of log-likelihood ratios asking whether settlements are better explained by H1 (entry price plus the benchmark mispricing) or H0 (the market is calibrated). Carry's H1 benchmark was seeded at +4.76¢ — the void number.

A decay detector inherits the credibility of its benchmark

The detector had been discriminating against a hypothesis that was already disproven. It would have read "edge intact" indefinitely on a sleeve with negative expected value, because the alternative it was testing was never real. A void benchmark launders a dead prior into an all-clear.

Worse, nulling the database value was not sufficient. The scanner does a set-if-null reseed from a hardcoded constant, so the void benchmark would have returned the moment any scanner started. Clearing state is only durable if you also remove whatever knows how to re-supply it.

07 What it cost, and what it bought

Direct cost: nothing — the sleeve finished up $12.20. The real cost was five weeks of research time spent building confidence in a number that could not have been true, plus the opportunity cost of a tennis sleeve built on the same defect.

What it bought:

  • A rule with teeth. Every entry condition must now be checked for decision-time computability before anything else. If the live system cannot compute the trigger, the backtest is not testing the strategy — and that gap is now the first thing looked for, not the last.
  • A correction to what replication means. Varying the sport, the season, or the parameter grid is not replication if the suspect component is held fixed.
  • An honest estimator of live edge. The realised ledger, with an interval that admits how little four losses can tell you, rather than a P&L number presented as a result.
  • The single-writer fence and the set-if-null lesson, both of which came out of this fortnight and both of which are the sort of defect that only shows up once real orders are in flight.
The point

A research process that only kills losing strategies is not a research process — it is a P&L stop. This one killed a strategy that was up 2.4%, on the evidence rather than the returns, thirteen days after the first fill. That is the behaviour the whole apparatus exists to produce, and it is the only result from this sleeve worth putting on a résumé.

08 Reproducibility

Every figure on this page traces to a source. The live numbers come from the strategy's own execution log — one row per attempt, fills and no-fills, with order IDs. Settlements were reconstructed independently by matching each market ticker to its game and deriving the winner from the final score, then cross-checked against Kalshi's own market endpoint on the subset it still serves: 18 of 18 agree, 0 disagreements. Backtest figures are quoted from the audit document dated 2026-07-11.

Methodology and the runnable statistical utilities — the corrected fee model, event-level block bootstrap, Wilson intervals for the loss-starved regime, and the CUSUM detector — are published in the companion repository. Live strategy parameters and platform source are not.
